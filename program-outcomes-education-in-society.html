Experience 1: EDU 301: Why Education?

  • Course: EDU 301: Why Education?
  • What I did: Examining the purpose, history, and evolution of education, and why different teaching methods work for different learners

This course asked me to step back from any particular classroom and ask what education is for in the first place. We looked at how education started, how it became integrated into society, how it has changed over time, and how it can still be changed. It also covered why different methods work for different people, whether children, adults, or learners with disabilities. What stayed with me most is the idea that how someone learns is tied to what learning means to them personally, which is not something a standardized system accounts for well.

How it connects to each outcome

OutcomeHow this experience shows it
ES.A Compare Various Functions of EducationThis is the outcome the course was built around. Comparing education across populations and settings was the work of the class, not a side effect of it. Studying how education is delivered differently to children, adults, and learners with disabilities showed me that there is no single function of education, only functions that different societies assign to it at different times. Learning the history also surfaced a bias of mine. I had assumed that the way school is organized now is the natural way to organize it, when in fact it is a set of choices made for particular reasons, many of which had little to do with how people actually learn.
ES.B Articulate Connections Between Values and OutcomesStudying how education evolved made the relationship between values and outcomes visible, because each shift in how education was organized reflected what a society valued at that moment. Tracing that pattern gave me a way to read any educational setting by asking what it is actually built to produce, rather than what it says it is for. That habit carried directly into my later coursework and my internship work.
ES.C Participate in Informed DebateThe question the course is named after does not have a settled answer. Whether education exists to prepare workers, to develop citizens, or to help individuals become themselves is genuinely contested, and people hold those positions for real reasons. Working through the course required me to take a position on what education should do while accounting for the fact that reasonable people disagree, which is the exact skill this outcome describes.

Experience 2: LSE 401: Designing Learning Environments for Inclusion

  • Course: LSE 401: Designing Learning Environments for Inclusion
  • What I did: Universal Design evaluation of Knott's Berry Farm, observing families with children on the spectrum and with disabilities in an open public space, followed by a business letter proposal to the organization

This was a real life experience outside of classroom expectations. Rather than studying inclusion in the abstract, I spent time watching how an open public space actually worked for families with children on the spectrum, with disabilities, or both. It gave me a different perspective on what those families may need in a public setting, and it made me reflect on how I could better assist a family in that situation.

How it connects to each outcome

OutcomeHow this experience shows it
ES.A Compare Various Functions of EducationA theme park is not a school, but it teaches constantly, and what it teaches about who belongs comes through its design rather than through instruction. Comparing it to a classroom showed me that formal settings have required accommodations while public spaces often treat them as optional. My own bias was that accessibility was mainly a school's responsibility. Watching families navigate a space that was not built with them in mind changed how widely I apply that question.
ES.B Articulate Connections Between Values and OutcomesThe park presents itself as family friendly, but I gathered specific evidence about how the space functioned and connected those features to the actual outcomes for particular families. The gap between the stated value and the lived outcome became the core of my proposal. What I concluded is that physical accommodations alone are not enough. Awareness, understanding, and patience from staff throughout the park matter just as much, and those are harder to build than a ramp.
ES.C Participate in Informed DebateThe business letter put me in a real disagreement with a real organization. Changes cost money, and the park could reasonably argue that its current accommodations are sufficient. I had to support my position with specific observed evidence rather than general appeals about inclusion, and write for an audience with the authority to say no.

Experience 3: EDU 484: Internship

  • Course: EDU 484: Internship
  • What I did: Human centered design research on violent tantrums among children in the foster system, including secondary research, interviews with youth, and surveys of caregivers

This was the experience where questions about equity stopped being abstract. I interviewed children whose access to a stable education was disrupted by circumstances they did not choose, and I gathered evidence about what actually helps them. It required me to build my understanding from the perspectives of the people affected rather than from assumptions about what they needed.

How it connects to each outcome

OutcomeHow this experience shows it
ES.A Compare Various Functions of EducationChildren in the foster system move placements, change schools, and carry trauma into classrooms built for continuity. Comparing their experience to that of children in stable homes showed me that education functions very differently depending on what a child brings into the room. My interviews also corrected a bias of mine directly. I had assumed younger children would struggle most, but I found that teenagers and younger children simply expressed and regulated emotion differently, and that trusted adults mattered more than any particular technique for both groups. That finding came from listening rather than from what I expected.
ES.B Articulate Connections Between Values and OutcomesI organized secondary research, survey data, and interview findings into a synthesis that connected stated values in child welfare, such as safety and stability, to the actual outcomes children reported. My survey found that about eighteen percent of respondents experienced frequent tantrums, which told me that a single uniform approach would not serve this population. Turning that evidence into an insight about tailored rather than standardized support is the work this outcome describes.
ES.C Participate in Informed DebateThis project sits inside a genuine debate about equity, specifically whether children with behavioral needs should be managed or supported, and who is responsible for providing that support. I took the position that intervention has to be individualized and trauma informed rather than uniform, and defended it with data. I also had to synthesize opposing perspectives from supervisors, caregivers, and clinicians, who did not always agree with each other. Revising my position based on their feedback, rather than defending my original idea, is what established credibility.

Reflection

Looking at these three experiences together, my development in this domain moved outward. I began by studying the question of what education is for, then went out into a real public setting to see who that education was actually reaching, and finally worked directly with the people affected to build something with them rather than for them. The most consistent lesson across all three was that my own assumptions were the first thing that needed examining, whether about where education happens, what it should accomplish for a particular child, or who is best positioned to say what works.
